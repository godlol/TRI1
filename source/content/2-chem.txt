@topic 1.1 | Moles and Molar Mass
- The **mole** connects the mass of a sample to the number of particles in it. One mole contains $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ particles, which is **Avogadro's number** ($N_A$).
- **Molar mass** in g/mol has the same numerical value as the average mass of one atom or molecule in amu.
- Use dimensional analysis to move between grams, moles, and particles. Use the subscripts in a formula to go from molecules to atoms.
= $n = \frac{m}{M}$ :: Moles from mass
= $\text{number of particles} = n \times N_A$ :: Particles from moles
! Check whether a question asks for atoms or molecules. One mole of $\ce{CO2}$ contains two moles of oxygen atoms.
? How many moles of oxygen atoms are in 22.0 g of $\ce{CO2}$ (molar mass 44.01 g/mol)?
a) 0.250 mol
b) 0.500 mol
*c) 1.00 mol
d) 2.00 mol
% $22.0/44.01 = 0.500$ mol of $\ce{CO2}$. Each molecule contains two oxygen atoms, so there is $1.00$ mol of O atoms. Stopping at 0.500 mol forgets the subscript.
? How many molecules are in 9.01 g of $\ce{H2O}$ (molar mass 18.02 g/mol)?
*a) $3.01 \times 10^{23}$
b) $6.02 \times 10^{23}$
c) $9.03 \times 10^{23}$
d) $5.43 \times 10^{24}$
% $9.01/18.02 = 0.500$ mol, and $0.500 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = 3.01 \times 10^{23}$ molecules. The value $9.03 \times 10^{23}$ is the number of atoms, not molecules.

@topic 1.2 | Mass Spectra of Elements
- The **mass spectrum** of an element has one peak for each **isotope**. A peak's position gives the isotope's mass, and its height gives the isotope's relative abundance.
- Isotopes of an element have the same number of protons and different numbers of neutrons.
- **Average atomic mass** is the abundance-weighted average of the isotope masses. It lies closest to the mass of the most abundant isotope.
= $\text{average atomic mass} = \sum (\text{isotope mass} \times \text{fractional abundance})$ :: Weighted average
! Do not take a simple average of the isotope masses. Convert each percent to a decimal and weight each mass.
? An element has two isotopes: one of mass 35.0 amu with 75.0% abundance and one of mass 37.0 amu with 25.0% abundance. What is the average atomic mass?
a) 35.0 amu
*b) 35.5 amu
c) 36.0 amu
d) 36.5 amu
% $35.0(0.750) + 37.0(0.250) = 26.25 + 9.25 = 35.5$ amu. The simple average, 36.0, ignores the abundances.
? The mass spectrum of an element shows two peaks: one at 10 amu with 20% relative abundance and one at 11 amu with 80% relative abundance. Which element is it?
a) Beryllium
*b) Boron
c) Carbon
d) Neon
% $10(0.20) + 11(0.80) = 10.8$ amu, which matches the average atomic mass of boron (10.81).

@topic 1.3 | Elemental Composition of Pure Substances
- A pure substance has a fixed composition. The **law of definite proportions** says a compound always contains the same elements in the same ratio by mass.
- The **empirical formula** is the lowest whole-number ratio of atoms. The **molecular formula** is a whole-number multiple of it.
- To find an empirical formula: treat percents as grams in a 100 g sample, convert to moles, divide by the smallest number of moles, and scale to whole numbers.
= $\text{mass percent of element} = \frac{\text{mass of element in 1 mol of compound}}{\text{molar mass of compound}} \times 100$ :: Mass percent
= $\text{multiple} = \frac{\text{molar mass}}{\text{empirical formula mass}}$ :: Molecular formula
! Convert masses to moles before taking ratios. A ratio of grams is not a ratio of atoms.
? A compound is 40.0% carbon, 6.7% hydrogen, and 53.3% oxygen by mass. What is its empirical formula?
a) $\ce{CHO2}$
*b) $\ce{CH2O}$
c) $\ce{C2H6O}$
d) $\ce{CH4O}$
% In 100 g: $40.0/12.01 = 3.33$ mol C, $6.7/1.008 = 6.6$ mol H, $53.3/16.00 = 3.33$ mol O. Dividing by 3.33 gives a ratio of 1 : 2 : 1.
? A compound has the empirical formula $\ce{CH2}$ (14.03 g/mol) and a molar mass of 84.16 g/mol. What is its molecular formula?
a) $\ce{C2H4}$
b) $\ce{C3H6}$
c) $\ce{C6H6}$
*d) $\ce{C6H12}$
% $84.16/14.03 = 6$, so the molecular formula is six empirical units: $\ce{C6H12}$.

@topic 1.4 | Composition of Mixtures
- A **mixture** contains two or more substances whose proportions can vary. A pure substance has one fixed composition.
- **Elemental analysis** measures the mass percent of each element in a sample. Comparing the result with the percent calculated for the pure compound shows whether the sample is pure.
- If the measured percent of an element is too low, the impurity contains a smaller percent of that element than the compound does (or none). If it is too high, the impurity contains a larger percent.
= $\text{mass percent of component} = \frac{\text{mass of component}}{\text{total mass of mixture}} \times 100$ :: Mixture composition
? Pure $\ce{NaCl}$ is 60.7% chlorine by mass. A sample labeled NaCl is found to be 58.0% chlorine by mass. Which impurity could account for the result?
a) $\ce{LiCl}$, which is 83.6% Cl by mass
b) $\ce{MgCl2}$, which is 74.5% Cl by mass
*c) $\ce{KCl}$, which is 47.6% Cl by mass
d) $\ce{AlCl3}$, which is 79.8% Cl by mass
% The sample has less chlorine than pure NaCl, so the impurity must have a lower mass percent of chlorine than 60.7%. Only KCl does.
? A 10.0 g mixture of sand and table salt is stirred into water and filtered. After drying, 2.5 g of sand remains on the filter paper. What is the mass percent of salt in the mixture?
a) 25%
b) 33%
*c) 75%
d) 80%
% The salt dissolved and passed through the filter: $10.0 - 2.5 = 7.5$ g of salt, and $7.5/10.0 = 75\%$.

@topic 1.5 | Atomic Structure and Electron Configuration
- An atom has a dense nucleus of protons and neutrons surrounded by electrons. Electrons are held by the attraction between opposite charges.
- **Coulomb's law**: the force between two charges grows with the size of the charges and shrinks as the distance between them grows.
- Electrons fill subshells from lowest energy up (**Aufbau principle**): $1s$, $2s$, $2p$, $3s$, $3p$, $4s$, $3d$, $4p$. An $s$ subshell holds 2 electrons, $p$ holds 6, and $d$ holds 10.
- **Core electrons** are in inner shells. **Valence electrons** are in the outermost occupied shell.
- To write a cation's configuration, remove electrons from the highest shell first. Transition metals lose their $4s$ electrons before their $3d$ electrons.
- **Ionization energy** is the energy needed to remove an electron from an atom or ion in the gas phase.
= $F_{\text{coulombic}} \propto \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}$ :: Coulomb's law
! $\ce{Fe^2+}$ is $[\text{Ar}]\,3d^6$. The two $4s$ electrons are removed, not two $3d$ electrons.
? What is the ground-state electron configuration of $\ce{Fe^2+}$?
a) $[\text{Ar}]\,4s^2\,3d^6$
b) $[\text{Ar}]\,4s^2\,3d^4$
c) $[\text{Ar}]\,4s^1\,3d^5$
*d) $[\text{Ar}]\,3d^6$
% Neutral iron is $[\text{Ar}]\,4s^2\,3d^6$. Forming the 2+ ion removes the two $4s$ electrons, which are in the highest occupied shell.
? Which element has the ground-state electron configuration $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^2\,3p^4$?
a) Silicon
b) Phosphorus
*c) Sulfur
d) Chlorine
% The superscripts add to 16 electrons, so the neutral atom has 16 protons. That is sulfur.
? According to Coulomb's law, which change increases the force of attraction between a nucleus and one of its electrons?
a) Increasing the distance between the electron and the nucleus
*b) Increasing the charge of the nucleus while the distance stays the same
c) Decreasing the charge of the nucleus while the distance stays the same
d) Adding more electrons between that electron and the nucleus
% Force is proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance. A larger nuclear charge at the same distance means a stronger attraction.

@topic 1.6 | Photoelectron Spectroscopy
- **Photoelectron spectroscopy (PES)** measures the energy needed to remove electrons from each subshell of an atom.
- Each peak is one subshell. The peak's **position** is the binding energy: electrons closer to the nucleus have higher binding energy, so $1s$ is always highest.
- The peak's **height** is proportional to the number of electrons in that subshell.
- Reading the peak heights in order gives the electron configuration and identifies the element.
- For the same subshell, an atom with more protons has a higher binding energy.
! Height tells you how many electrons. Position tells you how strongly they are held. Check which direction the energy axis runs before reading a spectrum.
? A photoelectron spectrum has five peaks. Listed from highest to lowest binding energy, their relative heights are 2, 2, 6, 2, and 3. Which element produced the spectrum?
a) Aluminum
b) Silicon
*c) Phosphorus
d) Sulfur
% The peaks correspond to $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^2\,3p^3$, a total of 15 electrons. The element with 15 protons is phosphorus.
? In photoelectron spectra, the $1s$ peak of oxygen appears at a higher binding energy than the $1s$ peak of nitrogen. The best explanation is that
a) oxygen has more electrons in its $1s$ subshell
*b) oxygen has more protons, so its $1s$ electrons are attracted to the nucleus more strongly
c) oxygen's $1s$ electrons are farther from the nucleus
d) oxygen's $1s$ electrons are shielded more than nitrogen's
% Both atoms have two $1s$ electrons at about the same distance. Oxygen's nucleus has a charge of 8+ instead of 7+, so by Coulomb's law the attraction is stronger.

@topic 1.7 | Periodic Trends
- Trends follow from Coulomb's law: the attraction depends on **effective nuclear charge** and on the distance of the valence electrons from the nucleus (the number of shells).
- **Atomic radius** decreases across a period (more protons pulling on the same shell) and increases down a group (more shells).
- **First ionization energy** increases across a period and decreases down a group. **Electronegativity** follows the same pattern; fluorine is highest.
- **Ionic radius**: cations are smaller than their atoms and anions are larger. In an isoelectronic series, the ion with the most protons is the smallest.
- **Successive ionization energies** rise steadily, then jump sharply when the first core electron is removed. The number of electrons removed before the jump is the number of valence electrons.
- **Electron affinity** generally becomes more negative across a period.
! Explain a trend with protons, shells, and attraction. Saying an atom "wants a full octet" or naming the trend is not an explanation.
? Chlorine atoms are smaller than sodium atoms. The best explanation is that
a) chlorine has more occupied electron shells than sodium
b) chlorine has fewer electrons than sodium
*c) chlorine has more protons attracting electrons in the same valence shell, so its effective nuclear charge is greater
d) chlorine's valence electrons are shielded from the nucleus more than sodium's
% Both atoms have valence electrons in the third shell with the same core shielding. Chlorine has 17 protons instead of 11, so its valence electrons are pulled in more strongly.
? Which of the following ions has the smallest radius?
a) $\ce{O^2-}$
b) $\ce{F-}$
c) $\ce{Na+}$
*d) $\ce{Mg^2+}$
% All four ions have 10 electrons. $\ce{Mg^2+}$ has the most protons (12), so it pulls the same electron cloud in the most tightly.
? The first five ionization energies of an element, in kJ/mol, are 578, 1817, 2745, 11577, and 14842. Which element is it?
a) Sodium
b) Magnesium
*c) Aluminum
d) Silicon
% The large jump comes between the third and fourth values, so three valence electrons are removed before a core electron. Aluminum has three valence electrons.
? Which list ranks the elements from lowest to highest first ionization energy?
a) $\ce{Li} < \ce{Na} < \ce{K}$
*b) $\ce{K} < \ce{Na} < \ce{Li}$
c) $\ce{Na} < \ce{K} < \ce{Li}$
d) $\ce{Li} < \ce{K} < \ce{Na}$
% Going down a group, the valence electron is in a higher shell, farther from the nucleus, and easier to remove. Potassium is lowest and lithium is highest.

@topic 1.8 | Valence Electrons and Ionic Compounds
- Elements in the same group have the same number of **valence electrons**, so they react in similar ways and form compounds with similar formulas, such as $\ce{NaCl}$ and $\ce{KCl}$.
- Typical ion charges: group 1 forms 1+, group 2 forms 2+, group 13 forms 3+, group 15 forms 3−, group 16 forms 2−, group 17 forms 1−.
- The formula of an ionic compound is the smallest whole-number ratio of ions that gives a total charge of zero.
> $\ce{Al^3+}$ and $\ce{O^2-}$ need a total of 6+ and 6−, so the formula is $\ce{Al2O3}$.
? What is the formula of the ionic compound formed from calcium and nitrogen?
a) $\ce{CaN}$
b) $\ce{Ca2N3}$
*c) $\ce{Ca3N2}$
d) $\ce{CaN2}$
% Calcium (group 2) forms $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and nitrogen (group 15) forms $\ce{N^3-}$. Three 2+ ions balance two 3− ions.
? An element M forms an oxide with the formula $\ce{M2O3}$. What is the most likely formula of its chloride?
a) $\ce{MCl}$
b) $\ce{MCl2}$
*c) $\ce{MCl3}$
d) $\ce{M2Cl3}$
% In $\ce{M2O3}$, three $\ce{O^2-}$ ions carry 6−, so each M is 3+. With $\ce{Cl-}$, a 3+ ion needs three chloride ions.

@unit chem-2 | Unit 2 | Compound Structure and Properties | 7–9% of exam

@topic 2.1 | Types of Chemical Bonds
- **Electronegativity** is an atom's ability to attract shared electrons. The difference in electronegativity between two bonded atoms sets the bond type.
- **Nonpolar covalent**: electrons shared equally (identical atoms or very similar electronegativity). **Polar covalent**: electrons shared unequally, giving partial charges. **Ionic**: electrons transferred, usually between a metal and a nonmetal. **Metallic**: valence electrons delocalized among metal atoms.
- The greater the electronegativity difference, the more polar the bond. The more electronegative atom carries the partial negative charge.
- Bonding is a continuum. The properties of a substance, such as conductivity when melted, are the best evidence for its bond type.
? Which bond is the most polar?
a) C–H
b) N–H
c) O–H
*d) F–H
% Fluorine is the most electronegative element, so the F–H bond has the largest electronegativity difference of the four.
? Which pair of elements is most likely to form an ionic bond?
a) C and O
*b) K and Br
c) N and Cl
d) S and O
% Potassium is a metal with low electronegativity and bromine is a nonmetal with high electronegativity, so an electron is transferred. The other pairs are nonmetals that share electrons.
? In a molecule of $\ce{HCl}$, the shared electrons are
a) shared equally by hydrogen and chlorine
*b) attracted more strongly to chlorine, giving chlorine a partial negative charge
c) transferred completely to chlorine, forming separate ions
d) attracted more strongly to hydrogen, giving hydrogen a partial negative charge
% Chlorine is more electronegative than hydrogen, so the bond is polar covalent with the partial negative charge on chlorine. The electrons are still shared, not transferred.

@topic 2.2 | Intramolecular Force and Potential Energy
- A graph of potential energy versus the distance between two nuclei has a minimum. The distance at the minimum is the **bond length**, and the depth of the minimum is the **bond energy**, the energy needed to break the bond.
- At shorter distances, repulsion raises the potential energy sharply. At large distances the atoms do not interact and the potential energy approaches zero.
- Higher **bond order** means a shorter and stronger bond: triple < double < single in length.
- For ions, Coulomb's law applies: attraction is stronger when the charges are larger and the ions are smaller (closer together).
~ pe-curve
! A stronger bond sits lower on the potential energy graph (a deeper well), not higher.
? On a graph of potential energy versus internuclear distance for two hydrogen atoms, the bond length of $\ce{H2}$ is
a) the distance at which the potential energy is zero
*b) the distance at which the potential energy is at its minimum
c) the distance at which repulsion between the nuclei is greatest
d) the distance at which the atoms stop interacting
% The molecule is most stable where attraction and repulsion balance, which is the bottom of the potential energy well.
? Based on Coulomb's law, which ionic compound has the strongest attraction between its ions?
a) $\ce{NaCl}$
b) $\ce{KBr}$
*c) $\ce{MgO}$
d) $\ce{CaO}$
% $\ce{MgO}$ and $\ce{CaO}$ have 2+ and 2− ions, which attract more strongly than 1+ and 1− ions. $\ce{Mg^2+}$ is smaller than $\ce{Ca^2+}$, so the ions in MgO are closer together.
? Which carbon–carbon bond is the shortest and takes the most energy to break?
a) The single bond in $\ce{C2H6}$
b) The double bond in $\ce{C2H4}$
*c) The triple bond in $\ce{C2H2}$
d) All three have the same length and strength
% More shared electron pairs between the same two nuclei pull them closer and hold them more strongly.

@topic 2.3 | Structure of Ionic Solids
- An ionic solid is a three-dimensional **lattice** of alternating cations and anions. The arrangement maximizes attraction between opposite charges and minimizes repulsion between like charges.
- Ionic solids have high melting points and are hard but **brittle**.
- They do not conduct electricity as solids because the ions are fixed in place. They conduct when melted or dissolved because the ions can move.
- There are no separate molecules. The formula gives the ratio of ions.
? Ionic solids are brittle because
a) ionic bonds are weak
b) their valence electrons are delocalized
*c) shifting one layer of ions brings ions of like charge next to each other, and they repel
d) the ions are held together only by London dispersion forces
% When a layer slides, cations line up with cations and anions with anions. The repulsion splits the crystal.
? A solid does not conduct electricity, but it conducts well after it is melted. The solid is most likely
a) $\ce{Cu}$
*b) $\ce{KBr}$
c) $\ce{SiO2}$
d) $\ce{C12H22O11}$
% Melting frees the ions of an ionic solid to carry charge. Copper conducts as a solid, and neither silicon dioxide nor sucrose contains mobile ions.

@topic 2.4 | Structure of Metals and Alloys
- In a metal, positive metal ions sit in a lattice surrounded by a **sea of delocalized valence electrons**.
- Mobile electrons explain electrical and thermal conductivity. Because the bonding is not directional, layers can slide without breaking the solid, so metals are **malleable** and **ductile**.
- **Interstitial alloy**: small atoms fill the spaces between larger metal atoms, as carbon does in steel. The result is usually harder and less malleable.
- **Substitutional alloy**: atoms of similar size replace some of the metal atoms, as zinc does in brass.
! Use atomic radius to decide the alloy type: much smaller atoms give an interstitial alloy; similar-sized atoms give a substitutional alloy.
? Steel is made by adding a small amount of carbon to iron. The carbon atoms fit into the spaces between the much larger iron atoms. Steel is best described as
a) a substitutional alloy
*b) an interstitial alloy
c) an ionic compound
d) a covalent network solid
% The small carbon atoms occupy the holes (interstices) between iron atoms instead of replacing them.
? Metals can be hammered into thin sheets without shattering because
a) metal atoms are held together by strong directional covalent bonds
b) metals have low melting points
*c) delocalized electrons let layers of metal ions slide past one another while staying bonded
d) ions of like charge repel when the layers shift
% The electron sea holds the ions together in any arrangement, so sliding a layer does not break the bonding.

@topic 2.5 | Lewis Diagrams
- Count the total valence electrons, adding one for each negative charge and subtracting one for each positive charge.
- Join the atoms with single bonds (least electronegative atom in the center, never hydrogen), complete the octets of the outer atoms, and place any remaining electrons on the central atom.
- If the central atom lacks an octet, turn lone pairs on outer atoms into double or triple bonds.
- Exceptions to the octet rule: hydrogen holds 2 electrons; boron and beryllium may have fewer than 8; elements in period 3 and below may hold more than 8.
? How many valence electrons are shown in the Lewis diagram of the sulfate ion, $\ce{SO4^2-}$?
a) 24
b) 30
*c) 32
d) 34
% Sulfur has 6, four oxygens have $4 \times 6 = 24$, and the 2− charge adds 2: $6 + 24 + 2 = 32$.
? In the Lewis diagram of $\ce{SF4}$, how many lone pairs are on the sulfur atom?
a) 0
*b) 1
c) 2
d) 3
% There are $6 + 4(7) = 34$ valence electrons. Four S–F bonds use 8 and the fluorine lone pairs use 24, leaving 2 electrons, one lone pair, on sulfur.

@topic 2.6 | Resonance and Formal Charge
- **Resonance** occurs when two or more valid Lewis diagrams differ only in where the electrons are placed. The real molecule is an average of them, called a resonance hybrid.
- In a resonance hybrid the electrons are **delocalized**, and the bonds involved are identical, with lengths and strengths between those of single and double bonds.
- **Formal charge** helps choose the best diagram. Prefer formal charges closest to zero, with any negative formal charge on the more electronegative atom. Formal charges add up to the overall charge.
= $\text{formal charge} = \text{valence electrons} - \text{lone-pair electrons} - \tfrac{1}{2}(\text{bonding electrons})$ :: Formal charge
! A molecule does not flip back and forth between resonance structures. It has one structure, the hybrid.
? What is the formal charge on the nitrogen atom in the nitrate ion, $\ce{NO3-}$?
a) −1
b) 0
*c) +1
d) +2
% Nitrogen forms four bonds (one double, two single) and has no lone pairs: $5 - 0 - \tfrac{1}{2}(8) = +1$.
? The carbonate ion, $\ce{CO3^2-}$, has three resonance structures. Which statement describes the actual ion?
a) It has one short C=O bond and two longer C–O bonds
b) It switches rapidly among the three structures
*c) All three carbon–oxygen bonds are identical, with a length between that of a single and a double bond
d) All three carbon–oxygen bonds are double bonds
% The electrons are delocalized over all three bonds, so each bond is the same, about one and one-third bonds.

@topic 2.7 | VSEPR and Hybridization
- **VSEPR**: electron domains around a central atom spread as far apart as possible. A domain is a lone pair or a bond; a double or triple bond counts as one domain.
- **2 domains**: linear, 180°, $sp$. **3 domains**: trigonal planar, 120°, $sp^2$; with one lone pair the shape is bent. **4 domains**: tetrahedral, 109.5°, $sp^3$; one lone pair gives trigonal pyramidal (about 107°); two give bent (about 104.5°).
- **5 domains**: trigonal bipyramidal (90° and 120°); lone pairs give seesaw, T-shaped, then linear. **6 domains**: octahedral (90°); lone pairs give square pyramidal, then square planar.
- Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs, so they squeeze the bond angles.
- A single bond is one **sigma (σ)** bond. A double bond is one σ and one **pi (π)** bond. A triple bond is one σ and two π bonds.
- A molecule is **polar** when its bond dipoles do not cancel. Symmetric shapes with identical outer atoms, such as $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{BF3}$, and $\ce{CCl4}$, are nonpolar.
! Count domains on the central atom from the Lewis diagram first. Name the molecular shape from the positions of the atoms only, not the lone pairs.
? What are the molecular geometry and approximate bond angle of $\ce{NH3}$?
a) Trigonal planar, 120°
*b) Trigonal pyramidal, about 107°
c) Tetrahedral, 109.5°
d) Bent, about 104.5°
% Nitrogen has four electron domains: three bonds and one lone pair. The atoms form a trigonal pyramid, and the lone pair pushes the angle a little below 109.5°.
? What is the hybridization of the carbon atom in $\ce{CO2}$?
*a) $sp$
b) $sp^2$
c) $sp^3$
d) The carbon atom is not hybridized
% Carbon has two electron domains because each double bond counts as one. Two domains correspond to $sp$ hybridization and a linear shape.
? Which molecule is nonpolar even though it contains polar bonds?
a) $\ce{NH3}$
b) $\ce{H2O}$
c) $\ce{CH3Cl}$
*d) $\ce{CCl4}$
% $\ce{CCl4}$ is tetrahedral with four identical C–Cl bonds, so the bond dipoles cancel. The other molecules are asymmetric and have a net dipole.
? How many sigma and pi bonds are in a molecule of $\ce{C2H2}$?
a) 2 sigma and 3 pi
*b) 3 sigma and 2 pi
c) 4 sigma and 1 pi
d) 5 sigma and 0 pi
% The structure is H–C≡C–H. Two C–H bonds and one bond of the triple bond are sigma (3 total); the other two bonds of the triple bond are pi.
? What is the molecular geometry of $\ce{XeF4}$?
a) Tetrahedral
b) Seesaw
*c) Square planar
d) Square pyramidal
% Xenon has six electron domains: four bonds and two lone pairs. The lone pairs sit opposite each other, leaving the four fluorine atoms in a square plane.

@unit chem-3 | Unit 3 | Properties of Substances and Mixtures | 18–22% of exam

@topic 3.1 | Intermolecular and Interparticle Forces
- **London dispersion forces** act between all particles. They come from temporary dipoles and grow stronger with more electrons (a more polarizable electron cloud) and with more surface contact between molecules.
- **Dipole–dipole forces** act between polar molecules.
- **Hydrogen bonding** is a strong dipole–dipole attraction: a hydrogen atom bonded to N, O, or F is attracted to a lone pair on N, O, or F in another molecule.
- **Ion–dipole forces** act between ions and polar molecules, as when a salt dissolves in water.
- Stronger intermolecular forces mean a higher boiling point, higher melting point, and lower vapor pressure.
! Boiling a molecular substance separates molecules from each other. It does not break the covalent bonds inside the molecules.
? All four compounds below are nonpolar. Which has the highest boiling point?
a) $\ce{CH4}$
b) $\ce{SiH4}$
c) $\ce{GeH4}$
*d) $\ce{SnH4}$
% Nonpolar molecules have only London dispersion forces. $\ce{SnH4}$ has the most electrons and the most polarizable electron cloud, so its dispersion forces are strongest.
? Water boils at 100 °C and hydrogen sulfide, $\ce{H2S}$, boils at −60 °C. The best explanation for the difference is that
a) the covalent bonds in water are stronger than those in hydrogen sulfide
b) water molecules have more electrons than hydrogen sulfide molecules
*c) water molecules form hydrogen bonds with each other, and hydrogen sulfide molecules do not
d) water has stronger London dispersion forces than hydrogen sulfide
% Hydrogen bonded to oxygen can hydrogen-bond; hydrogen bonded to sulfur cannot. Hydrogen sulfide actually has more electrons, so its dispersion forces are stronger, yet water still boils far higher.
? Pentane and 2,2-dimethylpropane both have the formula $\ce{C5H12}$. Pentane, a straight chain, boils at 36 °C. The compact, nearly spherical 2,2-dimethylpropane boils at 9.5 °C. The best explanation is that
a) pentane molecules are polar
b) pentane has more electrons
c) pentane forms hydrogen bonds
*d) the longer shape of pentane allows more surface contact between molecules, giving stronger London dispersion forces
% Both molecules are nonpolar with the same number of electrons. The elongated molecule has more area in contact with its neighbors, so its dispersion forces are stronger.

@topic 3.2 | Properties of Solids
- **Ionic solids**: ions held by strong electrostatic attraction. High melting point, brittle, conduct only when melted or dissolved.
- **Molecular solids**: separate molecules held by intermolecular forces. Low melting point, soft, do not conduct. Examples: ice, $\ce{I2}$, dry ice, sugar.
- **Covalent network solids**: atoms joined by covalent bonds throughout the whole solid. Very high melting point and very hard. Examples: diamond, $\ce{SiO2}$, $\ce{SiC}$.
- **Metallic solids**: metal ions in a sea of electrons. Conduct as solids and are malleable.
- **Graphite** is a network solid made of sheets. Each carbon bonds to three others, and the leftover electrons are delocalized, so graphite conducts and its sheets slide.
- **Crystalline** solids have an ordered, repeating arrangement. **Amorphous** solids do not.
? A solid has a melting point above 1600 °C, is extremely hard, and does not conduct electricity as a solid or as a liquid. The solid is most likely
a) ionic
b) metallic
c) molecular
*d) covalent network
% Very high melting point and hardness point to strong bonds throughout the solid. An ionic solid would conduct when melted and a metal would conduct as a solid, so it is a covalent network solid.
? Solid iodine, $\ce{I2}$, turns to vapor on gentle heating. The best explanation is that
a) the covalent bonds within $\ce{I2}$ molecules are weak
*b) only weak London dispersion forces between $\ce{I2}$ molecules must be overcome
c) iodine is an ionic solid with small ions
d) iodine atoms are held in a sea of delocalized electrons
% Iodine is a molecular solid. Changing phase separates whole molecules from each other, which requires overcoming only intermolecular forces.
? Graphite conducts electricity and diamond does not. The best explanation is that
a) graphite contains metal atoms
b) graphite contains mobile ions
*c) each carbon atom in graphite bonds to only three others, leaving delocalized electrons that can move through the sheets
d) the covalent bonds in diamond are weaker than those in graphite
% In diamond every valence electron is locked in a bond to one of four neighbors. In graphite one electron per carbon is delocalized across the sheet.

@topic 3.3 | Solids, Liquids, and Gases
- **Solid**: particles packed closely in fixed positions; they only vibrate. Fixed shape and volume.
- **Liquid**: particles close together but able to move past one another. Fixed volume, takes the shape of its container.
- **Gas**: particles far apart, moving constantly and randomly, with negligible attractions. Fills its container and is easily compressed.
- The solid and liquid phases of a substance have similar molar volumes because the particles are in close contact in both.
- The phase depends on the balance between intermolecular forces and the kinetic energy of the particles.
? Why are the densities of a substance's solid and liquid phases similar, while its gas phase is far less dense?
*a) Particles are in close contact in both the solid and the liquid but are far apart in the gas
b) Particles in the gas phase have less mass
c) Particles stop moving in the solid and liquid phases
d) Covalent bonds inside the molecules are stronger in the solid and liquid phases
% Density depends on how closely particles are packed. Solids and liquids are both condensed phases; in a gas most of the volume is empty space.
? A particulate diagram shows particles that are close together, arranged without a regular pattern, and able to move past one another. The diagram represents
a) a crystalline solid
*b) a liquid
c) a gas
d) an amorphous solid
% Close together but free to flow describes a liquid. In an amorphous solid the particles are also disordered, but they stay in fixed positions.

@topic 3.4 | Ideal Gas Law
- The ideal gas law relates pressure, volume, moles, and temperature. Temperature must be in **kelvin**.
- With moles fixed, use the combined gas law. Pressure and volume are inversely related; volume and pressure are each directly related to kelvin temperature.
- **Dalton's law**: the total pressure of a gas mixture is the sum of the partial pressures. Each partial pressure equals the gas's **mole fraction** times the total pressure.
- At STP (273 K and 1 atm), one mole of an ideal gas occupies about 22.4 L.
= $PV = nRT \qquad R = 0.08206\ \tfrac{\text{L·atm}}{\text{mol·K}}$ :: Ideal gas law
= $\frac{P_1 V_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2 V_2}{T_2}$ :: Combined gas law
= $P_{\text{total}} = P_A + P_B + \cdots \qquad P_A = X_A P_{\text{total}}$ :: Dalton's law
= $M = \frac{dRT}{P}$ :: Molar mass from density
! Convert Celsius to kelvin before using any gas law: $K = {}^\circ\text{C} + 273$.
? A 0.500 mol sample of an ideal gas occupies 12.3 L at 300. K. What is its pressure?
a) 0.100 atm
*b) 1.00 atm
c) 2.00 atm
d) 10.0 atm
% $P = \frac{nRT}{V} = \frac{(0.500)(0.08206)(300.)}{12.3} = 1.00$ atm.
? A container holds 2.0 mol of $\ce{N2}$, 1.0 mol of $\ce{O2}$, and 1.0 mol of Ar at a total pressure of 8.0 atm. What is the partial pressure of $\ce{N2}$?
a) 2.0 atm
*b) 4.0 atm
c) 6.0 atm
d) 8.0 atm
% The mole fraction of $\ce{N2}$ is $2.0/4.0 = 0.50$, so its partial pressure is $0.50 \times 8.0 = 4.0$ atm.
? A gas in a rigid, sealed container has a pressure of 1.0 atm at 27 °C. It is heated to 327 °C. What is the new pressure?
a) 0.50 atm
*b) 2.0 atm
c) 6.0 atm
d) 12 atm
% Convert to kelvin: 300 K and 600 K. At constant volume, pressure is proportional to kelvin temperature, so the pressure doubles. Using Celsius gives the wrong answer of 12 atm.
? A gas has a density of 1.25 g/L at 273 K and 1.00 atm. Which gas could it be?
a) $\ce{He}$
*b) $\ce{N2}$
c) $\ce{O2}$
d) $\ce{CO2}$
% $M = \frac{dRT}{P} = \frac{(1.25)(0.08206)(273)}{1.00} = 28.0$ g/mol, the molar mass of $\ce{N2}$.

@topic 3.5 | Kinetic Molecular Theory
- Gas particles are in constant, random motion. An ideal gas has particles of negligible volume, with no attractions between them and perfectly elastic collisions.
- Pressure comes from collisions of particles with the container walls.
- The **average kinetic energy** of gas particles depends only on the kelvin temperature. All gases at the same temperature have the same average kinetic energy.
- At the same temperature, lighter particles move faster on average.
- A **Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution** shows the spread of particle speeds. Raising the temperature makes the curve broader and flatter and moves its peak to higher speed.
= $KE_{\text{per molecule}} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ :: Kinetic energy
? Separate flasks of He and Ar are at the same temperature. Which statement is correct?
a) The Ar atoms have the greater average kinetic energy because they are heavier
b) The He atoms have the greater average kinetic energy because they move faster
*c) Both gases have the same average kinetic energy, and the He atoms have the greater average speed
d) Both gases have the same average kinetic energy and the same average speed
% Average kinetic energy depends only on temperature. Because $KE = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, the lighter helium atoms must move faster to have the same kinetic energy.
? When the temperature of a gas sample is increased, its Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution of molecular speeds
a) becomes narrower and taller and shifts toward lower speeds
b) keeps exactly the same shape but shifts toward higher speeds
*c) becomes broader and flatter and shifts toward higher speeds
d) does not change, because the number of molecules is constant
% At higher temperature the average speed rises and the speeds spread out more. The area under the curve stays the same, so the peak is lower.

@topic 3.6 | Deviation from Ideal Gas Law
- Real gases deviate from ideal behavior most at **low temperature** and **high pressure**, when particles are slow and close together.
- **Attractive forces** between particles make the measured pressure lower than the ideal prediction, because particles strike the walls with less force.
- **Particle volume** matters at very high pressure. The particles take up part of the container, so the measured pressure is higher than the ideal prediction.
- Gases behave most ideally at **high temperature** and **low pressure**. Small particles with weak attractions, such as He, come closest to ideal.
? Under which conditions does a real gas behave most like an ideal gas?
a) Low temperature and high pressure
b) Low temperature and low pressure
c) High temperature and high pressure
*d) High temperature and low pressure
% At high temperature the particles move too fast for attractions to matter, and at low pressure they are far apart, so their own volume is negligible.
? At low temperature, the measured pressure of a sample of $\ce{NH3}$ gas is lower than the pressure predicted by the ideal gas law. The best explanation is that
a) $\ce{NH3}$ molecules have no volume
*b) attractive forces between $\ce{NH3}$ molecules reduce the force of their collisions with the container walls
c) $\ce{NH3}$ molecules move faster than the ideal gas law predicts
d) collisions between $\ce{NH3}$ molecules are perfectly elastic
% Ammonia molecules attract each other strongly through hydrogen bonding. A molecule about to hit the wall is pulled back by its neighbors, which lowers the pressure.

@topic 3.7 | Solutions and Mixtures
- A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture: its composition is uniform throughout at the particle level. A **heterogeneous mixture** has regions with different compositions.
- Solutions can be solids (alloys), liquids, or gases (air).
- **Molarity** is moles of solute per liter of solution.
- Diluting a solution adds solvent but leaves the moles of solute unchanged.
= $M = \frac{\text{moles of solute}}{\text{liters of solution}}$ :: Molarity
= $M_1 V_1 = M_2 V_2$ :: Dilution
! Molarity uses liters of solution, not liters of solvent. Convert mL to L.
? What mass of $\ce{NaOH}$ (molar mass 40.00 g/mol) is needed to prepare 250. mL of a 0.200 M solution?
a) 0.0500 g
*b) 2.00 g
c) 8.00 g
d) 32.0 g
% Moles needed: $0.250\ \text{L} \times 0.200\ \text{mol/L} = 0.0500$ mol. Mass: $0.0500 \times 40.00 = 2.00$ g.
? What volume of 6.0 M $\ce{HCl}$ is needed to prepare 300. mL of 0.50 M $\ce{HCl}$?
a) 2.5 mL
*b) 25 mL
c) 100 mL
d) 3600 mL
% $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$ gives $V_1 = \frac{(0.50)(300.)}{6.0} = 25$ mL.

@topic 3.8 | Representations of Solutions
- A particulate drawing of a solution should show the correct relative numbers of solute particles and how the solute and solvent particles interact.
- Soluble ionic compounds separate into individual ions in water, in the ratio given by the formula. Molecular solutes such as sugar stay as whole molecules.
- Water molecules orient around ions: the **oxygen** end (partial negative) points toward cations, and the **hydrogen** ends (partial positive) point toward anions. These are ion–dipole attractions.
! Do not draw dissolved ionic compounds as molecules. $\ce{CaCl2}$(aq) is one $\ce{Ca^2+}$ ion and two separate $\ce{Cl-}$ ions, not $\ce{Cl2}$.
? In an accurate particulate drawing of $\ce{NaCl}$ dissolved in water, the water molecules nearest a $\ce{Na+}$ ion are oriented with
*a) their oxygen atoms pointing toward the ion
b) their hydrogen atoms pointing toward the ion
c) no particular orientation
d) one hydrogen atom and the oxygen atom equally close to the ion
% Oxygen is the partially negative end of a water molecule, so it is attracted to the positive sodium ion.
? Which statement describes a 0.20 M solution of $\ce{CaCl2}$, a soluble ionic compound?
a) $[\ce{Ca^2+}] = 0.20$ M and $[\ce{Cl-}] = 0.20$ M
*b) $[\ce{Ca^2+}] = 0.20$ M and $[\ce{Cl-}] = 0.40$ M
c) $[\ce{Ca^2+}] = 0.10$ M and $[\ce{Cl-}] = 0.20$ M
d) $[\ce{Ca^2+}] = 0.20$ M and $[\ce{Cl2}] = 0.20$ M
% Each formula unit releases one calcium ion and two chloride ions, so the chloride concentration is twice the calcium concentration.

@topic 3.9 | Separation of Solutions and Mixtures
- Separation methods use differences in intermolecular forces and physical properties.
- **Filtration** separates an undissolved solid from a liquid. It cannot remove a dissolved solute.
- **Evaporation** recovers a dissolved solid by removing the solvent.
- **Distillation** separates liquids with different boiling points. The component with weaker intermolecular forces vaporizes first.
- **Chromatography** separates components that are attracted differently to a **mobile phase** (the moving solvent) and a **stationary phase** (such as paper). A component attracted more to the mobile phase travels farther.
? In paper chromatography, a mixture of dyes is separated using a nonpolar solvent as the mobile phase and polar paper as the stationary phase. The dye that travels farthest up the paper is the one that is
a) most polar, because it is most attracted to the paper
*b) least polar, because it is most attracted to the solvent
c) made of the heaviest molecules
d) present in the greatest amount
% A less polar dye interacts weakly with the polar paper and dissolves well in the nonpolar solvent, so it moves with the solvent.
? Two miscible liquids have boiling points of 56 °C and 118 °C. The best way to separate them is
a) filtration
b) paper chromatography
*c) distillation
d) evaporating the mixture to dryness
% Distillation uses the difference in boiling points. The liquid that boils at 56 °C vaporizes first and is condensed and collected separately.

@topic 3.10 | Solubility
- Substances with similar intermolecular forces tend to dissolve in each other: "like dissolves like."
- Ionic and polar solutes dissolve in polar solvents such as water. Nonpolar solutes dissolve in nonpolar solvents such as hexane.
- A solute dissolves when solute–solvent attractions are about as strong as the solute–solute and solvent–solvent attractions they replace.
- Molecules that can hydrogen-bond with water, such as small alcohols, are very soluble. A longer nonpolar carbon chain lowers solubility in water.
? Which substance is most soluble in water?
a) $\ce{CH4}$
b) $\ce{CCl4}$
c) $\ce{C6H14}$
*d) $\ce{CH3OH}$
% Methanol has an O–H group that hydrogen-bonds with water. The other three molecules are nonpolar.
? Iodine, $\ce{I2}$, dissolves much better in hexane, $\ce{C6H14}$, than in water. The best explanation is that
a) iodine reacts chemically with hexane
b) iodine forms hydrogen bonds with hexane
*c) iodine and hexane are both nonpolar, so the London dispersion forces between them are similar in strength to the forces in the pure substances
d) water molecules are too small to surround iodine molecules
% Mixing iodine with water would mean breaking strong hydrogen bonds between water molecules and replacing them with weaker attractions. Hexane and iodine interact through similar dispersion forces.

@topic 3.11 | Spectroscopy and the Electromagnetic Spectrum
- Different regions of the electromagnetic spectrum cause different changes in molecules and atoms.
- **Microwave** radiation changes how molecules **rotate**.
- **Infrared** radiation changes how bonds **vibrate**. It is used to identify the types of bonds in a molecule.
- **Ultraviolet and visible** radiation move **electrons** between energy levels.
- From lowest to highest photon energy: microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet.
? Which pairing of radiation and molecular change is correct?
a) Microwave radiation and electronic transitions
*b) Infrared radiation and molecular vibrations
c) Ultraviolet radiation and molecular rotations
d) Visible light and changes in the nucleus
% Infrared photons match the energy spacing of bond vibrations. Microwaves cause rotations, and ultraviolet and visible light cause electronic transitions.
? A chemist wants to study the movement of electrons between energy levels in a dye molecule. Which region of the spectrum should be used?
a) Microwave
b) Infrared
c) Radio
*d) Ultraviolet and visible
% Electronic transitions require more energy than rotations or vibrations. Ultraviolet and visible photons supply it.

@topic 3.12 | Properties of Photons
- Light has wave properties: wavelength ($\lambda$) and frequency ($\nu$) are inversely related through the speed of light.
- Light also comes in packets of energy called **photons**. Higher frequency, or shorter wavelength, means more energy per photon.
- An atom or molecule absorbs or emits a photon only when the photon's energy equals the difference between two of its energy levels.
= $c = \lambda\nu \qquad c = 2.998 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$ :: Wave equation
= $E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda} \qquad h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J·s}$ :: Photon energy
! Convert nanometers to meters before calculating: $1\ \text{nm} = 10^{-9}\ \text{m}$.
? What is the frequency of light with a wavelength of 500. nm? ($c = 2.998 \times 10^{8}$ m/s)
*a) $6.00 \times 10^{14}\ \text{s}^{-1}$
b) $6.00 \times 10^{5}\ \text{s}^{-1}$
c) $1.50 \times 10^{2}\ \text{s}^{-1}$
d) $1.67 \times 10^{-15}\ \text{s}^{-1}$
% $\nu = \frac{c}{\lambda} = \frac{2.998 \times 10^{8}}{5.00 \times 10^{-7}} = 6.00 \times 10^{14}\ \text{s}^{-1}$. Leaving the wavelength in nanometers gives $6.00 \times 10^{5}$.
? What is the energy of one photon of light with a wavelength of 400. nm? ($h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}$ J·s, $c = 2.998 \times 10^{8}$ m/s)
a) $2.65 \times 10^{-40}$ J
b) $4.97 \times 10^{-28}$ J
c) $1.66 \times 10^{-27}$ J
*d) $4.97 \times 10^{-19}$ J
% $E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34})(2.998 \times 10^{8})}{4.00 \times 10^{-7}} = 4.97 \times 10^{-19}$ J.
? An electron in an atom drops from a higher energy level to a lower energy level. Which statement is correct?
a) A photon is absorbed, with energy equal to the energy of the higher level
*b) A photon is emitted, with energy equal to the difference between the two levels
c) A photon is emitted, with energy equal to the sum of the two levels
d) No photon is involved unless the electron leaves the atom
% The atom loses energy, and that energy leaves as a photon. Its energy equals the gap between the two levels, which fixes its frequency through $E = h\nu$.

@topic 3.13 | Beer–Lambert Law
- **Absorbance** is proportional to concentration. $\varepsilon$ is the molar absorptivity (how strongly the substance absorbs at that wavelength), $b$ is the path length, and $c$ is the concentration.
- With the same substance, cuvette, and wavelength, absorbance and concentration are directly proportional. A graph of absorbance versus concentration for known solutions (a calibration curve) is a straight line used to find an unknown concentration.
- Measure at the wavelength where the substance absorbs most strongly.
- Common errors: a smudged cuvette scatters light and raises the absorbance reading; water left in the cuvette dilutes the sample and lowers it.
= $A = \varepsilon b c$ :: Beer–Lambert law
? A 0.10 M solution of a colored compound has an absorbance of 0.30. A second solution of the same compound, measured in the same cuvette at the same wavelength, has an absorbance of 0.75. What is its concentration?
a) 0.025 M
b) 0.040 M
*c) 0.25 M
d) 0.40 M
% Absorbance is proportional to concentration: $c = 0.10 \times \frac{0.75}{0.30} = 0.25$ M.
? A student rinses a cuvette with distilled water and fills it with a colored solution without drying it or rinsing it with the solution first. Compared with the true value, the measured absorbance will be
a) higher, because water absorbs visible light strongly
*b) lower, because the solution in the cuvette is slightly diluted
c) the same, because absorbance does not depend on concentration
d) the same, because the path length is unchanged
% The leftover water lowers the concentration of the sample, and absorbance is proportional to concentration.
